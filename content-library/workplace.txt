# Original introductory Microsoft 365 and workplace courses; each section is a course.
@ Teams Collaboration Basics | Microsoft 365
Channels and decisions | A project decision is buried in a private chat while the rest of the team works from an older plan. | Record the decision and its owner in the appropriate shared project channel or approved record. | Use chat for a quick conversation and a shared workspace for information the team needs to find later. Include the decision, reason and next action without duplicating confidential material into a wider audience. | Start another private chat with a few more people. | Assume everyone will eventually hear about the decision.
Meeting preparation | A meeting invitation has no purpose or materials, and several attendees are optional contributors. | State the decision or outcome, share accessible preparation material, and invite the people needed for it. | A focused meeting gives participants a reason to prepare. Check file permissions before sending links, and distinguish required decision-makers from people who only need the notes. | Invite everyone to ensure no one is left out. | Send the preparation files only after the meeting ends.
Meeting follow-through | A discussion ends with several tasks but no agreed owners. | Record each action with an owner and due date in the approved shared workspace. | Meeting notes are useful when they support follow-through. Confirm decisions and unresolved questions, share them with the appropriate audience, and track changes in one place rather than creating competing versions. | Rely on each participant's memory. | Assign every task to the whole team without a named owner.
@ Shared Files with OneDrive and SharePoint | Microsoft 365
Choosing a shared location | A department depends on a document stored only in one employee's personal work area. | Move the team-owned record to the approved shared location with appropriate ownership and permissions. | Match storage to responsibility and lifecycle. Personal work storage can support individual drafts, while team records need continuity when people change roles or leave. | Email a separate copy to every team member each week. | Share the employee's sign-in so others can reach the original.
Working on one version | Four reviewers return different attachments with conflicting edits. | Use a shared document link with suitable editing permissions and version history. | Coauthoring reduces parallel copies, but it still needs clear ownership and review decisions. Check access and avoid broad public links when sharing the working version. | Rename every attachment final and choose the newest email. | Merge changes manually without retaining any review history.
Restoring a document | An important paragraph was accidentally removed from a shared document. | Review version history and restore or recover the relevant content through the approved document workflow. | Check the correct file and version before recovery, and coordinate with collaborators so useful later edits are not lost. Version history supports recovery but does not replace organizational backup and retention controls. | Overwrite the whole document with an old local copy without checking. | Ask every collaborator to stop using the file permanently.
@ Outlook and Clear Email | Microsoft 365
Useful requests | You need a colleague to approve a change, but your email only says please review. | State the requested decision, relevant context and response deadline clearly. | A concise request puts the action near the beginning and supplies enough information to decide. Use a descriptive subject and link to the controlled document rather than attaching unnecessary copies. | Send several reminder emails without clarifying the action. | Put the actual request only in an attachment filename.
Recipients and attachments | You are about to send a sensitive file and autocomplete selected a similar external name. | Verify the recipients, attachment and sharing permissions before sending. | Autocomplete is a convenience, not a directory approval. A brief final check catches misaddressed messages; if exposure occurs, report promptly rather than relying on message recall. | Send it and use recall if the wrong person replies. | Assume a familiar display name guarantees the right recipient.
Calendar clarity | A meeting includes people in different time zones and an unclear working-time assumption. | Use the calendar's time-zone-aware scheduling tools and confirm the intended local times. | Calendar systems can translate an instant across zones, but ambiguous written times and daylight-saving changes still confuse people. Include the purpose, location or meeting link, and correct date before sending. | Write 9 AM without specifying the date or zone. | Adjust the time manually for every attendee without checking calendar conversion.
@ Excel Data Essentials | Microsoft 365
Checking imported data | A spreadsheet import turns employee IDs with leading zeros into numbers. | Set the identifier field to a suitable text format during import and validate representative rows. | Identifiers are labels, even when they contain digits. Check types, dates, separators and missing values before analysis; formatting a damaged value afterward may not recover the original information. | Assume every digit-only column should be numeric. | Recreate missing zeros from memory without checking the source.
Sorting complete records | You want to sort a roster by department and select only that column. | Sort the entire table or expanded data range so each row remains a complete record. | Sorting one column independently can attach the wrong names, scores or dates to a person. Keep headers and record boundaries clear, then verify representative rows after the operation. | Sort just the department column to save time. | Copy names back manually after sorting without a reference.
Verifying formulas | A total looks reasonable, but new rows may fall outside its formula range. | Inspect the formula and included range, then reconcile a sample against the source data. | Plausible output is not evidence of correctness. Structured tables can help ranges grow consistently, but still review references, hidden rows and error values before using a result for decisions. | Accept the number because it resembles last month's result. | Replace the formula with a typed total so it cannot change.
@ Clear Documents and Presentations | Workplace skills
One clear purpose | A presentation contains many facts but no explicit decision or takeaway. | Define the audience's needed action and organize the content around that purpose. | Give each section a clear job: establish the issue, show relevant evidence and explain the next step. Remove material that does not help the audience understand or act. | Add more slides to cover every available detail. | Make the title more decorative without changing the structure.
Accessible content | A slide uses tiny text and color alone to distinguish two outcomes. | Use readable text, sufficient contrast and labels that communicate meaning without color alone. | Accessibility improves comprehension for varied devices and abilities. Use meaningful headings and descriptive text alternatives, and check reading order in the document's accessibility tools. | Assume the presenter can read every small label aloud. | Use more saturated colors as the only distinction.
Review and release | A document is ready for broad distribution but contains unresolved comments and tracked changes. | Complete the review and inspect the final distributed copy, including permissions and hidden review material. | Publication is a controlled handoff. Confirm the approved version, owner and audience, then verify that the exported or shared result contains what recipients should actually see. | Send the working draft and let recipients decide which edits are final. | Rename the file approved without checking its contents.
