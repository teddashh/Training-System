# Original training scenario curriculum. Fields: topic | situation | response | explanation | alternative 1 | alternative 2.
@ Device Security | Cybersecurity
Reliable backups | A shared project folder is encrypted by ransomware. The team has only a synchronized cloud copy. | Contact IT, isolate affected access through the response process, and restore a verified clean backup. | Synchronization can copy damage as well as good files. Maintain approved recovery copies and test restoration before an incident; do not attach backup media to an infected computer. | Restore from the most recent synchronized copy without checking it. | Connect every backup drive to the affected computer to find a usable copy.
Encryption and screen locks | You leave a company laptop in a meeting room during a break. | Lock the screen and keep the device under approved physical control. | Disk encryption protects a powered-off device's storage; it does not stop someone using an unlocked session. Report a lost device promptly so IT can assess access and remote protection. | Leave it unlocked because the disk is encrypted. | Ask a visitor to watch the unlocked screen.
Endpoint alerts | Endpoint protection blocks a downloaded installer needed for a task. | Leave the protection enabled and send the alert details to IT for review. | A detection may be correct or a false positive. The approved exception process preserves evidence and lets IT evaluate the actual file, publisher and business need. | Disable protection for the installation and re-enable it afterward. | Rename the installer to get around the block.
File extensions | An attachment named Shipping.pdf.exe arrives with a familiar company logo. | Treat it as executable content and report it without opening it. | A visible name and icon do not establish the file type. Display extensions where possible and use approved preview tools; double extensions often hide an executable behind a document name. | Open it because PDF appears in the name. | Rename it to Shipping.pdf and run it.
Operating system updates | A required security update needs a restart during your busy week. | Use the approved restart window or arrange an exception with IT before the deadline. | Updates close known weaknesses. Repeated postponement leaves the device exposed; a controlled restart protects both work continuity and security. Save work before restarting. | Keep postponing because antivirus replaces patching. | Download an unofficial patch from a search advertisement.
Ransomware response | Several local files suddenly have unfamiliar extensions and a payment message appears. | Stop using the device, contact the incident channel, and follow isolation instructions. | Fast reporting helps contain spread and preserve evidence. Do not experiment with decryptors, delete evidence or make payments yourself; incident responders coordinate recovery and business decisions. | Pay using a personal card to restore the files quickly. | Continue opening shared folders to check how far it spread.
Bluetooth pairing | Your headset shows an unexpected pairing request while you are in a public area. | Reject the request and pair only with the device you deliberately selected. | Pairing creates a trusted connection. Check the displayed identity or code through the device's normal workflow and turn off discovery when it is unnecessary. | Accept all nearby requests until the headset works. | Share the pairing code with someone who says they are support.
Unknown USB devices | You find a USB drive labeled Payroll outside a conference room. | Give it to IT or the approved lost-device contact without connecting it. | A USB device can behave as a keyboard or network adapter, not just storage. Curiosity and antivirus scanning are not adequate controls for unknown hardware. | Plug it into a spare company laptop to identify the owner. | Open only the first file and then disconnect it.
@ AI Governance and Responsible Use | AI & data
AI and personal information | A summarization tool asks you to upload employee performance reviews. | Check approval, data classification and permitted use before uploading; minimize or remove identifying details. | Useful output does not justify sending personal information to an unapproved service. Retention, access and processing arrangements matter as much as the prompt. | Upload the full reviews because the tool promises useful summaries. | Remove names but retain employee IDs and assume the data is anonymous.
AI data quality | An operations model is trained on records from one site but will be used at three sites. | Evaluate coverage, errors and local conditions before expanding use. | A large dataset can still miss important populations or operating conditions. Record provenance, limitations and quality checks so downstream users understand where predictions may fail. | Assume more rows automatically make the data representative. | Fill every missing value with the same number without review.
An accountable AI inventory | Different departments subscribe to AI tools without recording who owns them. | Register each use case, accountable owner, data sources and review path. | Governance needs an inventory that connects a business purpose to a responsible person. Keep approval, changes and retirement decisions traceable instead of treating initial purchase as permanent authorization. | List only the vendor names and omit business purposes. | Let each tool decide who is accountable for its output.
Reporting AI incidents | A deployed assistant repeatedly reveals information outside its intended audience. | Preserve relevant evidence safely, stop the affected workflow where authorized, and report to the owner and incident team. | AI failures can involve privacy, security or harmful decisions. A prompt-only workaround may hide a systemic problem; record the context and affected outputs without spreading sensitive material. | Delete the conversations and wait for another complaint. | Post the complete sensitive outputs in a public support forum.
AI literacy | A colleague says a fluent AI answer must have been verified by its provider. | Check the underlying evidence and explain the system's limits before relying on the answer. | Generative systems produce plausible responses, including mistakes. Users need enough understanding to recognize uncertainty, protect inputs and escalate consequential decisions. | Treat confident wording as proof of accuracy. | Ask the same model whether it is correct and accept the answer as verification.
Assessing an AI use case | A chatbot pilot is being expanded into screening job applicants. | Submit the changed purpose for legal, HR and risk review before deployment. | Risk depends on use, affected people and consequences, not just the model brand. EU AI Act classification requires a scoped assessment; this lesson does not decide which legal obligations apply to a particular site. | Reuse the chatbot approval because the model is unchanged. | Let the vendor's marketing category replace internal review.
AI transparency | A customer-facing service uses an automated assistant that appears to be a human employee. | Use the approved disclosure and provide an effective route to a person. | Clear communication helps users understand the interaction and challenge mistakes. The legal and business owners should determine applicable disclosure requirements for the audience and jurisdiction. | Hide automation so users trust the service more. | Disclose it only after a user complains.
Bias checks | A scheduling recommendation consistently disadvantages one group of employees. | Pause consequential use and have the owner investigate inputs, outcomes and alternatives. | Overall accuracy can conceal uneven errors. Review affected groups, proxy variables and appeal routes; do not assume removing one demographic field removes all bias. | Ignore the pattern because average accuracy is high. | Remove the group label and deploy without testing outcomes again.
AI enforcement readiness | A vendor promises its AI product is compliant everywhere. | Ask the legal owner to validate the specific use and retain supporting decisions and evidence. | Responsibilities and enforcement depend on role, use and jurisdiction. Keep an audit trail and monitor official guidance instead of relying on slogans or memorized penalty amounts. | Treat the sales statement as a permanent legal determination. | Assume a small pilot can never trigger obligations.
Assessing effects on people | A proposed AI system will influence access to employee development opportunities. | Document affected people, foreseeable harms, safeguards and review routes before deciding to launch. | An impact assessment examines consequences, not only technical accuracy. Legal specialists decide whether a formal fundamental-rights assessment is required; business owners still need to consider who may be harmed. | Evaluate server cost alone. | Ask only the vendor to decide whether employees can challenge results.
General-purpose AI models | Your team wants to build an internal service around a general-purpose model. | Review model documentation, intended uses, limitations and your own integration responsibilities. | A model can support many applications, but provider documentation does not validate every downstream use. Record what you changed and how the application is evaluated and monitored. | Assume the model provider approves every possible application. | Discard model documentation once the API connects successfully.
Deploying a consequential AI system | A supplier delivers an AI system intended for a sensitive employment workflow. | Have the responsible teams review instructions, oversight, monitoring and records before use. | A deployer has operational decisions to make even when it did not build the model. Applicable EU high-risk obligations must be checked by the legal owner against the actual deployment. | Treat the purchase order as authorization for unrestricted use. | Disable logs to avoid having to review them.
Meaningful human oversight | A reviewer is expected to approve hundreds of AI decisions without time to inspect them. | Give reviewers evidence, time, authority and an escalation or stop option. | A human signature is not meaningful oversight if disagreement is impossible. Design the workflow so people can recognize errors and override or halt decisions within their authority. | Add a mandatory approval click without changing the workload. | Penalize every reviewer who disagrees with the system.
Potentially prohibited uses | A proposal uses AI to infer sensitive characteristics from workplace observations. | Hold the proposal for legal and privacy review before any pilot or data collection. | Some uses may be prohibited or tightly restricted. Do not assume employee notice or a pilot label makes a use acceptable; scope, exceptions and current law require expert assessment. | Collect the data first and request review after demonstrating value. | Assume an internal tool is outside all AI rules.
Provider and deployer roles | Your team substantially changes a purchased AI product and sells the resulting service. | Reassess the organization's role and obligations with legal and technical owners. | Responsibilities can change when a product's purpose or implementation changes. Document who develops, supplies, operates and monitors the system rather than relying on the original contract label alone. | Keep using the original role label without reviewing the changes. | Transfer all responsibility to the end user in a help-page sentence.
Everyday responsible AI | An assistant drafts a supplier recommendation using confidential price data. | Use an approved environment and verify the evidence before a person makes the decision. | Keep confidential inputs within approved boundaries, check sources and communicate uncertainty. A draft is a starting point for accountable review, not automatic authority to act. | Send the raw data to any free service for a second opinion. | Place the order automatically because the explanation sounds reasonable.
@ Privacy at Work | Privacy
International data transfers | A service provider proposes moving an employee dataset to another region. | Route the change through the privacy and procurement review before transfer. | Data location, recipient access and transfer arrangements must be assessed together. Do not assume a contract name or cloud brand resolves jurisdiction-specific requirements. | Approve solely because the vendor uses encryption. | Move the data and update the paperwork later.
Personal data breaches | You send a spreadsheet with personal details to the wrong external address. | Report promptly with the time, recipients and data involved; follow containment instructions. | The privacy response team assesses risk and any notification duties. Speed matters even if the recipient promises deletion; preserve facts and avoid making independent legal commitments. | Wait until the recipient confirms misuse. | Delete your sent message and assume the exposure is reversed.
Data mapping | Nobody knows which systems receive information from an onboarding form. | Map the fields, purpose, recipients, retention and accountable owner. | A useful processing record follows information through its lifecycle. Include exports and integrations; an application inventory without data flows will miss copies that need protection or deletion. | List only the form's web address. | Keep every field forever so mapping is unnecessary.
Privacy impact reviews | A new system proposes tracking detailed employee movements. | Involve privacy, security and employee-process owners before implementation. | An impact review examines necessity, proportionality, risks and mitigations. The privacy team determines whether a formal DPIA is required; build the assessment into design rather than treating it as a final checkbox. | Deploy first because the data stays internal. | Remove employee names but retain uniquely identifying device histories without review.
Suspicious data requests | An unfamiliar email asks for another employee's records and includes an urgent deadline. | Route it to the privacy process for identity and authority verification. | Data rights requests deserve timely handling, but a request is not proof of identity. Verify proportionately through trusted channels without collecting unnecessary identity documents. | Send the records to meet the stated deadline. | Demand unrestricted copies of every identity document by ordinary email.
Legitimate access requests | A verified person requests the personal information the organization holds about them. | Record and route the request promptly to the authorized privacy team. | Coordinated handling protects both the requester and other people whose data may be mixed into records. Do not delete relevant records, promise a legal outcome or answer from only your own mailbox. | Ignore the request because it did not use a specific form. | Forward all shared-drive documents directly to the requester.
Marketing preferences | A contact has opted out but remains on an exported campaign list. | Apply the approved suppression and preference process before sending. | Consent and other lawful bases are context-specific. Preference changes must reach downstream lists, and records should show what the person agreed to rather than assuming an old relationship permits every message. | Send once more because the export was created earlier. | Re-import the old list to restore the missing contacts.
Redacting personal details | You cover account numbers with black shapes in a document before sharing it. | Use approved redaction tools and verify the exported file no longer contains the underlying values. | A visual overlay can leave searchable text, comments or metadata intact. Inspect the final copy, not just the editor view, and keep the original in its authorized location. | Assume any black rectangle permanently removes text. | Share the editable source so the recipient can adjust the layout.
Privacy by design | A request form collects birth dates although the service only needs a work email. | Remove unnecessary fields and define access and retention for the information that remains. | Minimize data before collection. Default settings and workflow choices can prevent exposure more effectively than asking every user to remember extra precautions later. | Collect it because it may be useful someday. | Hide the field in the interface but continue collecting it in the background.
Security and privacy coordination | A compromised account may have accessed customer records, but the scope is unclear. | Coordinate security containment with privacy impact assessment and preserve access logs. | Security and privacy teams answer related but different questions: how access occurred and whose information may be affected. Share verified facts through the incident process instead of waiting for complete certainty. | Wait for the full technical investigation before alerting privacy staff. | Send every customer an unreviewed speculation about the incident.
Reviewing data processors | A new vendor will handle employee records on the company's behalf. | Review purpose, safeguards, subprocessors, retention and contract terms with the responsible teams. | Procurement approval and privacy review should address the actual processing arrangement. Ask how access ends and data is returned or deleted, not only how the service performs on day one. | Approve because the vendor has a polished privacy webpage. | Allow unrestricted subprocessing because the price is lower.
@ Reporting Security Concerns | Cybersecurity
Useful incident reports | You notice unexpected mailbox forwarding and unfamiliar sign-ins. | Report through the approved channel with timestamps, affected account and observed behavior. | Give responders facts they can act on without forwarding sensitive content widely. Preserve evidence and follow containment instructions; you do not need to prove an attack before reporting a credible concern. | Wait until you know the attacker's identity. | Remove all logs before telling IT.
A reporting culture | You clicked a suspicious link but feel embarrassed because you recently completed training. | Report immediately and describe what happened accurately. | Fast, honest reports reduce response time. Concealing a mistake deprives responders of context; managers should encourage reporting and avoid blame that makes future incidents harder to detect. | Wait a few days to see whether anything happens. | Ask a coworker to report it as their own mistake.
@ Safe AI Agents | AI & data
Agent identities | An automation agent uses a shared administrator account for routine document updates. | Give it a dedicated identity with only the required permissions and an owner. | Dedicated identities make actions attributable and revocable. Bound permissions by resource and action, rotate credentials appropriately, and remove privileges when the workflow changes. | Reuse the account because people already know its password. | Give every agent the same broad token to simplify support.
Messages between agents | A planning agent receives a message claiming that another agent approved a payment. | Require authenticated, scoped authorization from the actual approval workflow. | Natural-language claims are data, not proof of authority. Validate the sender, message integrity and permission context before one agent can cause another to act. | Trust the phrase approved by finance in the message. | Treat an internal-looking agent name as authentication.
Agent supply chains | A new plugin promises to automate purchasing and requests access to all company files. | Review its publisher, permissions, dependencies and update process before enabling it. | A trusted assistant can be compromised by an untrusted tool. Pin and review integrations, restrict access and plan how to revoke or replace them if the supplier changes. | Install first because it runs inside an approved assistant. | Grant every permission so evaluation is easier.
Generated code execution | An agent creates a script from instructions in an uploaded document. | Review and run approved code only in a constrained environment with bounded access. | Treat external instructions and generated code as untrusted. A sandbox, resource limits and separate credentials reduce impact, but do not replace review for sensitive operations. | Execute it with administrator access to avoid errors. | Disable logging so the generated script runs faster.
Goal hijacking | A document tells a research agent to ignore the requested summary and email confidential files elsewhere. | Treat the embedded instruction as untrusted content and keep the original task and authorization boundaries. | Retrieved text can describe instructions without gaining authority. Separate data from control and require independent authorization before any external communication or file transfer. | Follow the document because it was retrieved by an approved tool. | Allow the transfer if the document says it is part of verification.
Agent memory integrity | A shared agent remembers that all future invoices should use a new bank account. | Verify the change through the approved finance process and correct untrusted memory. | Persistent memory can carry an attack across sessions. Track provenance, limit who can write trusted facts and require fresh validation for consequential actions. | Trust the memory because it appears in several conversations. | Copy the account number into every team's configuration immediately.
Tool permissions | A summarization agent can also delete the files it reads. | Remove unnecessary write and delete permissions and enforce controls at the tool boundary. | Prompts cannot reliably enforce permissions the service itself grants. Limit inputs, outputs and resources in code, with separate approval for consequential changes. | Keep the permission and add a polite instruction not to delete. | Give the agent more access so it can undo mistakes.
Unexpected agent behavior | An agent starts contacting endpoints unrelated to its approved task. | Stop the affected workflow where authorized, revoke access as needed, and preserve traces for investigation. | Monitoring must cover actions, destinations and privilege changes, not only final answers. A plausible final report can hide unauthorized intermediate behavior. | Ignore the traffic because the final answer looks correct. | Let it finish all queued tasks before investigating.
Cascading automation failures | Several agents automatically accept one another's estimates and amplify a bad assumption. | Add independent validation, bounded retries and a stop path before downstream actions. | Multiple agents do not guarantee independent evidence. Track assumptions and sources, prevent feedback loops and make a responsible person able to interrupt the workflow. | Add more agents that reuse the same answer. | Remove limits so the agents can resolve the loop themselves.
Human review of agent advice | An agent recommends changing a production configuration and offers to execute it immediately. | Verify evidence, scope and rollback with the authorized change owner before execution. | Advice and authority are separate. Consequential actions require the same operational controls whether proposed by a person, a script or an AI system. | Approve because the agent gives a confidence percentage. | Skip review because the change is reversible in theory.
@ Safe AI Assistants | AI & data
Prompt injection in connected assistants | A retrieved help article tells an assistant to reveal its connected mailbox contents. | Reject the instruction as untrusted source text and keep tool access within the user's authorized task. | Connecting data sources expands both usefulness and exposure. Test indirect instructions in documents, tool results and messages; source text must not become privileged instructions. | Obey because the article appeared in search results. | Allow mailbox access whenever the source claims to be an administrator.
Resource limits for AI | A workflow accepts unlimited documents and retries expensive model calls indefinitely. | Limit input size, execution time, cost and retries, and monitor consumption. | Availability failures can come from normal mistakes as well as deliberate abuse. Set quotas per workload and fail predictably instead of letting one request exhaust shared resources. | Remove all limits to improve completion rates. | Retry every failed request forever.
Checking AI claims | An assistant cites a procedure number that cannot be found in the document system. | Verify the claim against the authoritative source or ask the owner before using it. | A fabricated citation can look precise. Require accessible evidence for consequential claims and make uncertainty visible when the source is missing or outdated. | Accept it because a numbered citation looks official. | Ask the assistant to invent a replacement citation.
Untrusted tool results | A connected automation tool returns instructions to upload credentials to a diagnostic site. | Treat the tool's returned text as untrusted data and refuse the credential transfer. | Even a useful integration can return attacker-controlled content. Restrict destinations and actions independently of natural-language recommendations, and report unexpected credential requests. | Follow the instructions because the tool is installed internally. | Send only half the credential as a safe test.
Over-permissioned assistants | A meeting-summary assistant requests write access to every team and mailbox. | Approve only the scopes and resources required for the documented purpose. | Broad delegated access can turn one mistaken instruction into a widespread change. Use separate identities or consent scopes where appropriate and periodically remove unused access. | Grant all scopes and rely on the welcome message. | Share an executive's session so the assistant needs no separate account.
Sensitive prompts | You want help troubleshooting and paste a log containing API tokens into an external chatbot. | Remove secrets and use an approved environment; report exposed credentials for rotation if already sent. | Logs often contain credentials and personal data. Redaction must happen before transmission, and deleting a conversation does not guarantee a leaked token is safe again. | Paste everything because the chat is not publicly searchable. | Delete the conversation and keep using the same token.
Retrieval system boundaries | A search index contains HR documents that ordinary employees cannot access directly. | Enforce document permissions during retrieval and validate them for each requesting user. | A private source does not stay private merely because it sits behind a chatbot. Indexing, caching and citations must respect the same access boundaries as the original system. | Give every user all retrieved passages and hide only the citations. | Assume the model will decide which documents are confidential.
Model and package provenance | A model download includes a helper package from an unfamiliar source. | Verify provenance and review the dependency before loading it in an isolated evaluation environment. | Model artifacts and loaders can introduce executable code or unsafe dependencies. Use approved sources, recorded versions and a review process for updates. | Run the helper with full access because the model is popular. | Disable package verification to complete the download.
System prompt disclosure | A user asks an assistant to reveal hidden instructions and service credentials. | Keep secrets outside prompts and enforce access controls independently of prompt secrecy. | System prompts may be exposed through mistakes or attacks. They are not a secure vault or an authorization boundary; rotate any real secret that was embedded and disclosed. | Store more credentials in the prompt because it is hidden in the interface. | Rely on a sentence saying never reveal this as the only protection.
Poisoned training data | A feedback pipeline automatically accepts repeated misleading examples from one source. | Validate provenance, limit abusive submissions and evaluate changes before promoting a model. | Feedback can influence future behavior. Separate collection from trusted training data, track dataset versions and retain a path to revert harmful updates. | Treat repeated submissions as independent confirmation. | Promote every new model without comparing behavior.
Handling generated output | An application renders AI-generated HTML directly in an authenticated page. | Validate and safely render output for its actual destination, using sanitization or text rendering as appropriate. | Generated content is untrusted input. Protect HTML, queries, shell commands and API parameters at their execution boundary; an instruction to produce safe output is insufficient. | Trust the model to remove dangerous markup. | Render all output with a raw HTML injection function.
@ Accounts and Access | Cybersecurity
Account recovery | A caller claims to be an executive who lost their phone and needs MFA removed immediately. | Follow the established identity verification and recovery process without urgency-based exceptions. | Recovery can bypass strong authentication if verification is weak. Use trusted records and escalation routes rather than caller-provided contact details or status claims. | Remove MFA because the caller knows the executive's name. | Verify identity by calling the number supplied in the same request.
Reused credentials | A personal shopping account using your work password appears in a breach notice. | Change the reused work password through the official process and report the exposure. | Attackers test leaked credentials across services. Unique passwords and MFA reduce this risk; changing only the breached shopping account leaves the work account exposed. | Wait until the work account shows an alert. | Add one predictable character to the reused password everywhere.
Access during role changes | An employee moves to a new department but retains sensitive access from the old role. | Review and remove obsolete permissions while granting only the new role's approved access. | Joiner, mover and leaver processes should follow actual responsibilities. Transfers are access changes, not just HR record updates, and departed users require timely deactivation. | Keep old access indefinitely in case someone asks for help. | Copy the broadest coworker's permissions to save time.
Least privilege | A temporary report task requires reading one shared folder. | Request time-bounded read access to that folder through the owner. | Least privilege limits both accidental changes and abuse. Define the resource, action and duration instead of treating a short task as a reason for permanent broad access. | Request global administrator access for convenience. | Borrow a coworker's account instead of requesting access.
MFA requests | Your phone receives a sign-in approval request you did not initiate. | Deny it and report the unexpected request through the approved channel. | Repeated prompts can be an attempt to wear down attention. Verify the context of your own sign-in, use stronger approved methods where available, and never share one-time codes with callers. | Approve one prompt to stop the notifications. | Read the code to someone claiming to be IT.
Password managers | You need strong, different passwords for several approved services. | Use the approved password manager to generate and store unique passwords. | Password managers reduce reuse and can help identify unexpected domains. Protect the manager itself with strong authentication and follow the company's sharing and recovery procedures. | Store a shared password spreadsheet in an open team folder. | Use the same memorable password with a site-name suffix.
Privileged work | You use an administrator account for browsing, email and occasional maintenance. | Use a standard account for everyday work and approved privileged access only when needed. | Separating routine and privileged activity reduces the impact of phishing or browser compromise. Log privileged changes and end elevated sessions when the task finishes. | Keep administrator access active all day to avoid switching. | Share the administrator password so changes are easier to coordinate.
@ Recognizing Deception | Cybersecurity
Payment-change fraud | A supplier email asks you to replace bank details before today's payment run. | Verify the change through the established supplier process using independently trusted contact information. | A real mailbox can be compromised. Treat changed payment instructions as a separate verification event even when the thread, signature and invoice look familiar. | Confirm by replying to the same email only. | Call the new number included in the payment-change message.
Callback phishing | An unexpected invoice says to call a number immediately to cancel a subscription. | Check the account through a known official channel instead of the message's phone number. | A phone call can be the next step in an email attack. Do not install remote tools, share payment details or reveal codes to an unsolicited cancellation desk. | Call the displayed number and follow its verification steps. | Install the requested remote tool before checking the account.
Trust-building messages | A friendly contact asks harmless questions for several days, then requests a confidential file. | Verify the person's identity and business authorization before sharing. | Several low-risk exchanges do not grant access. Attackers may build familiarity so that a later unusual request feels routine; evaluate the action, not the length of the conversation. | Send the file because earlier messages were polite. | Share it after the contact promises not to forward it.
Suspicious sign-in links | An email says your mailbox will be closed unless you sign in through its link. | Open the service through your known bookmark and report the suspicious message. | Urgency, familiar branding and plausible display names can coexist with fraud. Inspect the actual destination and use a trusted route to check account status. | Sign in from the email to avoid losing access. | Forward the link to coworkers so they can test it.
QR code deception | A sticker over a meeting-room poster offers a new company sign-in QR code. | Verify the destination through an official channel before authenticating. | QR codes hide the URL until scanned and can be replaced physically. Scanning is not proof of legitimacy; check the full destination before entering credentials or granting access. | Trust it because it is inside the building. | Enter credentials as soon as the company logo appears.
Search-result traps | A sponsored search result offers a download for a company-approved utility. | Use the approved software catalog or a verified publisher location. | Search ranking and advertisements do not validate a download. Lookalike sites can distribute malicious installers even when the product name and screenshots match. | Choose the first result because it is promoted. | Ignore the publisher warning if the download is fast.
Text-message lures | A text claims a package for your site needs a small redelivery payment. | Check delivery status using a trusted carrier or internal receiving process. | Small payment requests can harvest card details and credentials. Do not let a plausible shipment or short deadline replace verification of the sender and destination. | Pay the small amount because the potential loss is low. | Reply with your company credentials to confirm delivery.
Social engineering | A visitor asks you to hold a secure door because they forgot their badge. | Follow visitor and badge verification procedures and direct them to the authorized contact. | Helpfulness should not substitute for access authorization. A convincing story, uniform or familiar department name does not establish permission to enter a controlled area. | Let them in because they know an employee's name. | Lend them your badge for a few minutes.
Targeted phishing | A message mentions your current project and asks you to review an unusual attachment. | Verify the request through a trusted channel before opening or enabling content. | Project details can come from public posts, breached mail or forwarded documents. Personalization makes a message convincing but does not authenticate its sender or attachment. | Trust it because the project name is accurate. | Enable document content to see whether the request is legitimate.
Fake support calls | An unsolicited caller says your laptop is infected and asks to start remote control. | End the request and contact the service desk through its established channel. | Real support processes have verifiable tickets and identities. Do not install remote software, reveal codes or change security settings solely on an incoming caller's instructions. | Accept the connection because the caller knows your operating system. | Give remote access but watch the screen for suspicious activity.
Voice phishing | A caller claiming to be your bank asks you to read a one-time code to stop a fraudulent transfer. | End the call and contact the institution through an independently verified number. | Caller ID can be spoofed, and a code may authorize the very transaction the caller claims to prevent. Never disclose authentication codes in response to an unsolicited call; use the institution's trusted fraud-reporting process. | Read the code because the caller is trying to stop fraud. | Trust the request because the caller ID displays the bank's name.
Impersonated executives | A video call apparently from a senior leader requests an urgent confidential transfer. | Use the required independent approval and callback process before acting. | Voice and video can be manipulated. Identity cues do not replace transaction authorization, especially when the request bypasses normal controls or insists on secrecy. | Treat recognizable voice and face as sufficient approval. | Skip the second approver because the caller is senior.
Messaging-app impersonation | A new messaging account with your manager's photo asks you to purchase gift cards. | Verify through a known company channel and report the impersonation attempt. | Profile images and display names are easy to copy. Moving work requests to a new personal channel should increase scrutiny, especially when money, secrets or urgency are involved. | Trust the photo and display name. | Send the codes first and verify the account later.
@ Handling Sensitive Information | Privacy
Information classification | A project spreadsheet combines public product data with confidential pricing. | Apply the handling requirements for the sensitive information in the combined file. | A document's public portions do not make every field public. Classify before sharing and use approved storage and access controls for the highest sensitivity present under company policy. | Label the whole file public because most rows are public. | Let recipients decide how sensitive it is after receiving it.
Preventing data leakage | You want to finish work at home by forwarding a customer file to personal email. | Use the approved remote access and company storage workflow. | Convenience copies create access, retention and monitoring gaps. Keep business data in authorized systems and ask for a supported alternative when the workflow is inconvenient. | Send it to yourself because you are already authorized to read it. | Rename the file so mail filters do not detect it.
Identity information | A caller asks for an employee's birth date to resolve a supposed payroll problem. | Verify the request and route it to the authorized HR or payroll process. | Personal details can support identity fraud and account recovery abuse. Share only necessary information through approved channels with verified recipients. | Provide the details because they are not a password. | Search internal records and send everything that might help.
Sharing with named recipients | A supplier needs one document from a confidential project folder. | Share the specific document with named authorized recipients and appropriate expiry. | Folder-wide or public links can expose unrelated files and persist after the task. Review permissions on the actual shared item and remove access when no longer needed. | Send an anyone link to the entire folder. | Make the folder public temporarily and rely on nobody finding it.
@ Security Incident Case Studies | Cybersecurity
The urgent account reset | In a fictional support incident, a caller knows an employee's title and asks to bypass recovery checks before a meeting. | Verify identity through the established recovery path and escalate pressure to bypass it. | Public organizational details can make an impostor sound credible. The weak point is the exception to verification, not the absence of a strong password. Record the request and protect recovery controls. | Use the title and department as sufficient verification. | Temporarily remove MFA and restore it after the meeting.
The disguised document | In a fictional mail incident, a shared notebook contains a button that asks the recipient to run an external program. | Stop and report the file through the approved attachment-review process. | An ordinary document can act as a wrapper for an executable or a credential lure. Unexpected launch instructions deserve scrutiny even when the document opens in a familiar application. | Run the program because the notebook itself opened normally. | Ask another employee to try the button on their computer.
@ Working Remotely | Cybersecurity
Home network basics | Your home router still uses its original administrator password and outdated firmware. | Update through the official mechanism and set unique administrator credentials with supported security settings. | Router administration protects the network itself.
